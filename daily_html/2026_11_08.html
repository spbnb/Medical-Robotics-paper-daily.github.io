Locating Contact on Tendon-Driven Continuum Robots From Tendon Measurements Alone via Constrained Moving Horizon Estimation

Abstract (English): Tendon-driven continuum robots can reach through constrained spaces, but contact along their bodies is invisible to the sensors at their motors. We show that this is a structural limitation rather than a tuning problem: the map from contact forces to tendon measurements has a null space of dimension at least seven, so no estimator that leaves the contact support free can locate contact. When the obstacle geometry is known, however, non-penetration and complementarity make the contact force locally identifiable, and we prove that the resulting system with unknown contact inputs is locally exponentially strongly detectable. We present a constrained moving horizon estimator (MHE) that encodes these conditions and runs in real time as one quadratic program per sample, and we give a local, mode-wise robust stability result for it. In a finite- element benchmark of a two-section robot pressing and sliding on a wall, the estimator located contact to 0.9 mm and estimated contact force to 0.22 N, compared with 65 mm and 0.72 N for a tuned extended Kalman filter (EKF) with state projection. The estimator tolerated millimeter- level errors in the obstacle position and unmodeled wall friction, under which the projected EKF failed. Under randomized model mismatch over 40 trials, including unmodeled tendon friction, it remained better in force and location in every trial. Without contact, the MHE offered no advantage over the EKF, which confirms that its benefit comes from the contact constraints. This report extends a letter submitted to IEEE Control Systems Letters with complete proofs and additional experiments.

GRAC: Graph-Based Retrieval-Augmented Captioning for Surgical Scene Description

Abstract (English): Purpose: Surgical operative reports support procedural documentation, surgical training, performance assessment, and workflow review. However, extracting meaningful information often requires extensive manual review and clinical expertise. Automated surgical scene descriptions could reduce this burden by leveraging recurring surgical activities and prior knowledge. Methods: We propose GRAC (Graph-Based Retrieval-Augmented Captioning), a framework that represents surgical scenes as graphs capturing relationships among instruments, actions, and anatomical structures. Given an input scene graph, GRAC learns to retrieve relevant graph-caption pairs from training data. It compares instrument-tissue interactions between retrieved examples and the input scene, using comparison features to condition retrieved-caption representations during generation. Results: On the EndoVis-18 benchmark, GRAC achieved a CIDEr-D of 5.88, compared with 4.23 for SGT and 3.36 for the strongest conventional Transformer baseline. Exact instrument-action-target triplet-set accuracy reached 52.6%, compared with 32.7% for SGT and 26.2% for the strongest conventional baseline. These results demonstrate improvements in language quality and factual agreement. On TORS, GRAC achieved a CIDEr-D of 5.06, compared with 3.80 for the strongest baseline. Conclusion: GRAC demonstrates that combining structured surgical scene representations with retrieved examples improves surgical scene captioning and produces more contextually meaningful descriptions.

Authors: Rochak Dhakal, Chen Zhao, Soup Cobb, Xin Qian, Xiaofeng Yang, Weihua Zhou

Can Agentic Systems Close the Gap in Radiology with Production AI Tools?

Abstract (English): Radiology AI has developed along two paths: specialized models that perform well on narrow tasks but cover few clinical findings, and generalist models that address a broader range of tasks but remain prone to hallucination and inaccurate measurements. Combining the two through agentic systems offers a route towards comprehensive radiology reporting, but published systems are tied to one modality or body part. We describe a radiology agentic framework that standardizes how an orchestrator calls modular tools to produce a structured report, and use it to measure what such a system can do with the tools from FDA-cleared radiology AI devices. Report generation is simulated on a large multi-modality report corpus, with the orchestrator given either a catalog of finding classes consolidated from the cleared devices, each available on the modalities it is cleared for, or an unlimited catalog in which any class can be requested, and planning either from the clinical indication or from the target report. Tool outputs are simulated from the reference report, so the comparison isolates tool availability and orchestration from tool error. With the cleared catalog and the indication alone, the orchestrator recovers under a third of the clinically actionable findings; recovery improves substantially with the unlimited catalog, and further when the plan is written from the target report. Attributing every missed finding to a cause shows that most are classes no cleared device offers for the study's modality, and that a third are classes or tools that were offered and not called. Adding the most frequently missing tools closes most of the gap to the unlimited catalog on held-out reports, whereas a smaller orchestrator with the unlimited catalog gives up the whole gain. Tool availability and effective orchestration are thus complementary constraints on comprehensive AI-assisted radiology reporting.
